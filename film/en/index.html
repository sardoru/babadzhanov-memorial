Opening

0:04 A silkworm lives for about a month. It eats nothing but the leaves of the mulberry tree, and in four weeks it grows some ten thousand times heavier. Then, in three days, it wraps itself in a single unbroken thread: a thousand metres of silk, sometimes more. Across Russian Turkestan, families raised silkworms at home for centuries. A survey of the region published in 1913 describes how the women who did the work carried the eggs in little bags inside their clothes, to hatch them with the warmth of their own bodies. By 1940, the Uzbek Soviet republic was producing half of all the silk cocoons in the Soviet Union. Behind that figure stood millions of mulberry trees, and the people who had learned how to grow them. In one family photograph, on an office desk among papers and inkwells, stand two glass jars full of cocoons. The desk belonged to a man who began life as a herd-boy in a village on the edge of the steppe.

I. Ikan

1:19 This is Ikan. The photograph was taken around 1871, for the Turkestan Album: mud walls, flat roofs, a caravan of camels passing by. Ikan was a farming village of Uzbeks, some twenty-five kilometres from the old city of Turkestan, on the road to Tashkent. Turkestan itself was a place of pilgrimage. Above its low houses rose the domes of the mausoleum of Khoja Ahmed Yasawi, a Sufi poet and saint of the twelfth century, whose shrine was built on the orders of Timur. Ikan had a place of its own in the history books. In December 1864, a Kokand army and a company of Ural Cossacks fought for three days outside the village, in one of the battles of the Russian conquest of Central Asia. Gulam Babadjanov was born in Ikan in 1907, into a family of dehqans, peasant farmers.

2:17 What we know of his childhood comes from a short entry in a book about the region's notable people. Entry number fifteen, printed beside his portrait, gives the bare facts. His father died when Gulam was one year old. His grandfather raised him until he was eight, and then his mother, until he was eleven. From 1918, the boy worked for other people: he herded their animals and helped with the sowing and the harvest. In the winter months, when there was no work in the fields, he studied at a newly opened school. Those were years of revolution, civil war and hunger in Turkestan. Schools of the new kind were only just reaching the villages, and for a boy without a father, one of them opened a door.

II. The Road to Tashkent

3:09 In 1922, at the age of fifteen, he set out for Tashkent, the capital of his own province and of the whole Turkestan republic, some two hundred kilometres to the south. There he entered the preparatory class of the Uzbek men's Institute of Education, known as the inpros. Despite its name, it was not a university but a college for teachers. A Tashkent directory of 1926 lists it: six classes, two of them preparatory, at the corner of Lenin and Engels streets. By official Soviet count, the state schools on Uzbek soil grew from a hundred and sixty in 1913 to almost two thousand by 1928, and every one of them needed teachers. Gulam went through all six years, and graduated in 1928.

4:01 While he studied, the map around him was redrawn. In 1924, Soviet Turkestan was divided along national lines. Tashkent and Samarkand became part of the new Uzbek Soviet republic; Turkestan and Ikan were assigned to the autonomous republic of the Kazakhs, within Soviet Russia. From then on, his home village and the city where he would make his life lay in different republics. He was not the only young man from Ikan to take this road. The poet Mirtemir, born there three years after him, studied at the same institute in Tashkent and then went on to Samarkand, as Gulam did. In 1928, Samarkand was the capital of Uzbekistan, and its new pedagogical institute was drawing students from across the region. Exactly what Gulam studied there, the documents found so far do not say.

III. The Mulberry

5:02 Silk depends on a tree. A silkworm will eat only the leaves of the mulberry: in its short life a single worm eats twenty to twenty-five grams of leaf, three quarters of it in its last stage before it spins. A family's silk harvest was only as good as the leaves it could pick. In the 1860s, a disease of the silkworm called pébrine nearly destroyed the silk industry of Europe, and Italian merchants travelled as far as Bukhara in search of healthy eggs. By the early twentieth century, the old local breeds of Turkestan were giving way to imported ones, and egg stations had begun to examine the moths under the microscope. Silk was becoming a science.

5:47 In 1927, a research institute for silk opened in Tashkent: the Central Asian Sericulture Research Institute. Its mulberry specialist was Professor Alexander Fedorov, the author of books on the mulberry and its breeding, who tested Japanese varieties of the tree on the institute's experimental farm. In the family's memory, Fedorov was Gulam's teacher. The tree became Gulam's subject.

6:14 By the middle of the 1930s he was writing about it himself. The national bibliography of Soviet Uzbekistan lists two booklets under his name, both in Uzbek, printed in the Latin alphabet that the language used from the late 1920s until 1940. The first, in 1934, was on growing mulberry saplings, issued in five thousand copies by the Komsomol's farm technical station. The second, in 1935, was a handbook of thirty-five pages on growing tall-stemmed mulberry trees, written for collective farmers, for brigade leaders on state farms and in mulberry nurseries, and for the managers of silk-growing. They were practical books, for people who worked the land.

7:02 The republic was planting mulberries by the million. A decree of 1940, signed by Molotov and Stalin, counted thirty-seven million trees, set new targets, and noted that some two thousand incubators had replaced the old way of hatching the eggs. A few months later, cutting down a mulberry tree without the government's permission became a criminal offence, and for every tree felled, ten young ones had to be planted. Silk had become a matter of state.

IV. Eleven Months

7:37 Then the family's story breaks. In 1938, at the height of the Great Terror, an anonymous letter was enough to end a career, or a life. His family remembers that Gulam Babadjanov was arrested that year on a false denunciation, and spent eleven months in prison. In Uzbekistan, the terror of 1937 and 1938 swept away a large share of the republic's managers and specialists, and thousands were shot. Silk science was not spared: one of Tashkent's leading silk professors had already spent two years in prison, on a false charge of spying for Japan. In November 1938, Moscow ordered the mass arrests to stop, and in the following year tens of thousands of people were released when their cases were dropped. The poet Mirtemir, from Ikan, had himself been arrested in 1932, and soon released. A fair and careful judge, the family says, dismissed the charges against Gulam, and he came home. His name is not in the Memorial or Open List databases of the repressed; someone released without a sentence often is not.

8:50 In 1939, he completed his postgraduate studies; he went on to defend a dissertation and become a Candidate of Agricultural Sciences, and later a docent. His field remained sericulture, and the mulberry.

V. Silk for the Front

9:11 In June 1941, war reached the Soviet Union. Factories, institutes and people were evacuated to Central Asia; Moscow's Timiryazev Agricultural Academy itself spent two years in Samarkand. A parachute factory from Tushino, near Moscow, worked in Tashkent until 1944, and Soviet-era figures credit Uzbekistan with some three hundred and thirty thousand parachutes for the front. Silk was scarce: the army's standard troop parachute of 1941 had a canopy of cotton percale. Soviet histories say that Uzbekistan's silk mills wove cloth for parachutes, and by their count the republic delivered fifty-four thousand tonnes of cocoons to the state over the four war years. Silk was a strategic material, and the people who knew how to grow it were needed. In the autumn of 1941, the Tashkent Agricultural Institute ran crash courses for district silk agronomists. His family remembers Gulam's war as work on silk for the front, for parachutes; the state later awarded him the medal For Valiant Labour in the Great Patriotic War.

VI. The Director

10:28 The institute he would lead had been founded on 26 May 1930, when the agricultural faculty of the Central Asian State University became a school of its own. In 1934 it took the name it would keep for decades: the Tashkent Agricultural Institute. Part of it had grown out of a sericulture institute, and silk was taught there from the beginning. In 1940, the same decree on silk ordered its sericulture faculty to be strengthened, to train at least seventy-five silk agronomists a year from 1944.

11:05 At the institute he rose step by step: head of a department, then deputy director for research and teaching, and from 1952, its director. The family's photographs show him at work. He stands at the telephone in his office, under a painted frieze. He sits at his desk, with a poster of the plant breeder Ivan Michurin on the wall, the official emblem of Soviet agricultural science in those years. He sits over papers with two colleagues, the two jars of cocoons between them. In the laboratory, he stands among students at their microscopes. Several of them still wear army tunics: young men back from the front, studying to be agronomists. From 1947, the institute published its own scientific proceedings.

11:54 He took students out into the fields as well. In the photographs he walks among young trees, examines a budding shoot, and holds up a tall sapling for two students. In a parade photograph from about 1950, the institute's column marches under its own banner. By official figures, the republic still raised about half of all Soviet silk cocoons: in 1953, more than thirteen thousand tonnes. He sat on the academic council of the sericulture research institute, was elected to the Tashkent city soviet and to the city's Party committee, and was deputy chairman of the republic's society for spreading political and scientific knowledge. For his work he received the Order of the Red Banner of Labour and the Order of the Badge of Honour. He had come to this desk from a herd-boy's childhood, by way of a teachers' college and the science of the mulberry. He had learned his science from the ground up, and he taught it the same way.

VII. 20 May 1955

12:59 Gulam Babadjanov died on 20 May 1955. The obituaries speak of a short illness; his family remembers an operation that failed. He was still in his forties. Two days later, the republic's Russian-language newspaper, Pravda Vostoka, and the Uzbek-language Tashkent daily printed his obituary, signed by a group of comrades, with notices of mourning from the city's Party committee, the city soviet, the Ministry of Agriculture and the sericulture research institute. Six days after his death came the twenty-fifth anniversary of the institute's founding.

13:40 He left a widow, Habiba Mirsagatova, a schoolteacher, and seven daughters, the youngest still small children. Habiba raised them, and the eldest, Elmira, helped her bring up the others. All seven went on to higher education. Among them were an endocrinologist, an ophthalmologist and a cardiologist; a philologist; a chemical engineer; and teachers of music and of language. Two of them earned doctoral degrees.

14:11 Little has been found in print so far: two small handbooks on mulberry trees, a few lines in a book of the region's people, and an obituary in two newspapers. The rest survives in photographs: an office, a laboratory, a field, a banner in a parade. And it survives in the people he taught. Each spring, in the silk villages, the mulberry trees are stripped of their leaves to feed the silkworms; and each year they grow them back. Gulam Babadjanov's life began in a village on the edge of the steppe, and ran through the classrooms and laboratories of Tashkent, to seven daughters, and their children, and theirs.

About the film

The Mulberry Tree was made in 2026 for this memorial. It is built from the Babadjanov family’s photographs and from archival records: a 1926 Tashkent directory, the Uzbek national bibliography, Soviet statistics and decrees on silk, and his obituaries in Pravda Vostoka and Toshkent haqiqati of 22 May 1955 (National Library of Uzbekistan).
